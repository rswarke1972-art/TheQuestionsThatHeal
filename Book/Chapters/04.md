# The Walls We Build

(The following afternoon, the college campus was quieter than usual.

Clouds drifted lazily across the sky, casting moving shadows over the garden where Professor Vinay and Rudhika had met every day that week.

For the first time...

neither of them carried a psychology textbook.

Only Rudhika held a small notebook against her chest.)

(Vinay was already sitting on the wooden bench beneath the banyan tree.

When he saw Rudhika approaching, he smiled.)

**Professor Vinay**: Right on time.

(Rudhika smiled faintly as she sat beside him.)

For a while...

neither of them spoke.

There was no awkwardness.

Only silence.

The kind of silence that exists between two people who don't feel the need to fill every moment with words.

A cool breeze stirred the leaves overhead.

Somewhere nearby, a wind chime hanging outside the horticulture department rang softly.

Finally...

Vinay spoke.

**Professor Vinay**: Yesterday I promised that today wouldn't be about theories.

It would be about you.

(Rudhika nodded.)

She looked down at her notebook.

Its pages were filled with observations from the previous days.

Yet today...

she hadn't written a single word.

**Professor Vinay**: May I ask you something?

**Rudhika**: (Looking up.) Of course.

**Professor Vinay**: Why do you always sit alone after class?

(Rudhika blinked.)

She hadn't expected that question.

**Rudhika**: I...

I don't know.

**Professor Vinay**: Really?

Or have you simply never thought about it?

(Rudhika remained quiet.)

She searched for an answer.

At first...

nothing came.

Then...

small memories began surfacing.

Eating lunch alone.

Watching other students from a distance.

Leaving class before anyone could start a conversation.

Politely declining invitations.

Always choosing the corner seat.

Always.

**Rudhika**: (Softly.)

I guess...

it's become a habit.

**Professor Vinay**: Habits usually have beginnings.

(Rudhika looked away.)

(Vinay didn't continue.

He simply waited.

The silence wasn't uncomfortable.

It was patient.)

After nearly a minute...

Rudhika finally spoke.

**Rudhika**: Professor...

have you ever noticed how some stray dogs behave?

(Vinay looked at her with curiosity.)

**Rudhika**: If they've always been loved...

they'll happily run toward anyone.

But if they've been beaten over and over again...

even someone trying to feed them has to earn their trust.

(Vinay didn't interrupt.)

(Rudhika gave a small, almost embarrassed smile.)

**Rudhika**: I think...

people can become like that too.

(Those words seemed to surprise even her.

She had never described herself that way before.)

**Professor Vinay**: That's a thoughtful observation.

Do you think you've stopped trusting people...

or have you stopped expecting kindness from them?

(Rudhika felt the question settle somewhere deep inside her.)

She wanted to answer immediately.

But she couldn't.

Because she wasn't sure.

For several moments...

she simply stared at the grass beneath her feet.

Then...

very quietly...

she spoke.

**Rudhika**: Professor...

I never really knew my parents.

(Vinay's expression softened.

He didn't say he was sorry.

He didn't interrupt.

He simply listened.)

**Rudhika**: They died in an accident when I was still a toddler.

I don't remember their faces.

I don't remember their voices.

Sometimes...

I wonder what it would have felt like...

to remember being hugged by them.

(The words came slowly.

As if they had been waiting years to be spoken.)

**Rudhika**: My elder sister raised me.

She was still a child herself.

But somehow...

she became everything.

She wasn't just my sister.

She became my family.

(She smiled faintly.)

Everything gentle in my life...

came from her.

(The smile slowly disappeared.)

**Rudhika**: We lived with our grandparents.

People always say grandparents spoil their grandchildren.

(Oddly...

that sentence made Rudhika laugh.

But there was no joy in it.)

**Rudhika**: That wasn't our story.

(Vinay remained completely still.

He could sense that what came next carried years of silence.)

(Rudhika took a slow, trembling breath.)

She looked at the empty page of her notebook.

**Rudhika**: There was one day...

when I learned...

that sometimes the people who are supposed to protect you...

can become the people you're most afraid of.

(She closed the notebook.)

Her fingers trembled ever so slightly.

(Vinay noticed.

He didn't ask her to continue.

He didn't tell her to "be strong."

He simply said,)

**Professor Vinay**: We have all the time you need.

(Rudhika looked at him.

For the first time in years...

she believed him.)

She drew another slow breath.

Then...

she began.

(Rudhika looked at the notebook resting in her hands.

She slowly traced a finger along its blank page.

When she finally spoke...

her voice was barely above a whisper.)

**Rudhika**: I think...

I should start with him.

Our cousin.

(For a brief moment, she wondered if she should even continue.

Then she remembered Professor Vinay's words.

*"We have all the time you need."*

She took another breath.)

**Rudhika**: My sister and I lived with our grandparents after our parents died.

There was also our cousin.

He was around my age.

Maybe a little older.

Everyone adored him.

Whatever he did...

someone had an excuse for it.

If my sister or I made a mistake...

it became a crime.

(Vinay listened without interrupting.)

**Rudhika**: When I was little...

I didn't understand favoritism.

I just thought...

maybe I wasn't trying hard enough.

Maybe if I behaved better...

they would love me too.

(She gave a quiet laugh.)

Children are strange.

They always think they are responsible for the weather inside a house.

(Vinay made a mental note of the sentence but didn't stop her.)

**Rudhika**: One afternoon...

my grandparents had gone out.

My sister wasn't home.

It was just me...

and him.

She had asked me to fold the freshly washed clothes.

I was happy to do it.

I wanted everything to be neat before they returned.

(She smiled faintly.)

I folded every piece carefully.

One by one.

I still remember checking each shirt twice...

because I didn't want anyone to complain.

(Her smile disappeared.)

Then...

without saying a word...

he picked up the pile of clothes...

and threw them over the balcony.

(The memory seemed to return with startling clarity.)

Rudhika stared at the ground.

Almost as though she were seeing the scattered clothes again.

**Rudhika**: I panicked.

I ran downstairs as fast as I could.

Some clothes had fallen into the dust.

Some were tangled in the plants.

I picked up every single one.

I dusted them with my hands.

I thought...

if I hurried...

maybe no one would know.

(She swallowed.)

When I came back upstairs...

he was waiting.

(For the first time since beginning her story...

her hands started trembling.)

**Rudhika**: He smiled.

Not because he was happy.

Because he knew something I didn't.

He stood in front of me and said...

"Good girl.

Now hand them to me again."

(Vinay remained silent.)

**Rudhika**: I begged him not to.

I told him Grandmother would be angry.

I told him we'd both get into trouble.

He only laughed.

Every time I pulled the clothes back...

he pulled harder.

Like it was a game.

Like my fear was entertaining.

(Her breathing became slower.)

**Rudhika**: I don't even remember deciding to do it.

My hand just...

moved.

I slapped him.

(The words hung in the air.)

**Rudhika**: It wasn't because I hated him.

It was because...

for one second...

I wanted the game to stop.

(She closed her eyes.)

Instead...

everything became worse.

He slapped me back immediately.

I remember falling.

I remember the ceiling spinning.

And then...

I remember lying on the floor.

Crying.

Not because of the slap.

Because I already knew...

what was going to happen next.

(Vinay's expression changed ever so slightly.)

He wasn't shocked.

He was listening with complete attention.

Rudhika continued.

**Rudhika**: A little while later...

my grandparents came home.

The clothes were still dirty.

He reached them before I could say a single word.

He told them I had thrown the clothes myself.

He told them I had hit him for no reason.

(She paused.)

I tried to explain.

I really tried.

But no one asked me what happened.

No one looked at me.

Their minds had already decided.

(The garden had become remarkably quiet.)

Even the sounds of students in the distance seemed to fade.

Rudhika looked down at her hands.

They had stopped trembling.

Not because the memory hurt less.

But because she had finally spoken it aloud.

**Rudhika**: That day...

my sister came between me and them.

She covered me with her own body.

If she hadn't...

I don't know what would have happened.

(Silence.)

A long silence.

Not empty.

Respectful.

Professor Vinay did not rush to comfort her.

He knew that some stories should be honored before they are answered.

Rudhika looked toward the old banyan tree.

For years...

she had carried this memory alone.

Today...

for the first time...

someone had listened without judging.

And somehow...

that changed the memory just a little.

(The breeze carried a few dry leaves across the stone pathway.

Neither of them spoke.

Several minutes passed.

Professor Vinay didn't seem interested in filling the silence.

He simply remained there.

Present.

Listening.)

(Rudhika wiped the corner of one eye before the tear could fall.)

**Rudhika**: I was fourteen.

Old enough to know what was happening.

Old enough to remember every second of it.

(She let out a quiet laugh.)

Sometimes people think painful memories disappear with time.

Mine became clearer.

(Vinay nodded gently.)

**Professor Vinay**: Memories tied to strong emotions often do.

Especially the ones our minds believe are important for survival.

(Rudhika looked toward the ground.)

**Rudhika**: After that...

I changed.

I stopped arguing.

I stopped explaining.

Whenever someone blamed me...

I just stayed quiet.

Because...

what was the point?

No one had listened that day.

Why would anyone listen later?

(Vinay listened carefully.)

**Rudhika**: I stopped trusting compliments too.

If someone was kind...

I kept waiting for them to become cruel.

If someone smiled...

I wondered what they wanted.

It felt...

safer to expect the worst.

(She looked embarrassed.)

**Rudhika**: That's probably why I stay away from people now.

It's easier to be alone.

If you don't let anyone close...

they can't hurt you.

(Professor Vinay folded his hands in his lap.)

He remained thoughtful for a moment.

Then he asked quietly,

**Professor Vinay**: May I ask you something?

**Rudhika**: Of course.

**Professor Vinay**: Imagine a fourteen-year-old girl came to my office tomorrow.

She sat exactly where you're sitting now.

And she told me the same story you just told me.

Every detail.

(He paused.)

**Professor Vinay**: Would you think she deserved what happened?

(Rudhika answered almost instantly.)

**Rudhika**: No.

**Professor Vinay**: Would you call her weak?

**Rudhika**: Never.

**Professor Vinay**: Would you tell her she should have known better?

Rudhika frowned.

**Rudhika**: No.

She was only trying to stop someone from tormenting her.

(Vinay nodded.)

**Professor Vinay**: Then let me ask one last question.

Why does that kindness disappear...

when the fourteen-year-old is you?

(Rudhika opened her mouth to answer.

Nothing came out.)

For the first time...

she realized there were two versions of compassion inside her.

One for everyone else.

And another...

far harsher one for herself.

(Vinay didn't interrupt her thoughts.)

He knew some questions should echo before they're answered.

After nearly a minute, Rudhika spoke.

Very softly.

**Rudhika**: Because...

if I admit I didn't deserve it...

then I have to admit something even more frightening.

**Professor Vinay**: What is that?

(Her eyes filled again.)

**Rudhika**: That the people who were supposed to protect me...

chose not to.

(The words seemed to drain years of weight from her shoulders.)

Vinay didn't rush to respond.

Instead, he looked up at the branches of the banyan tree.

**Professor Vinay**: When we cannot make sense of another person's cruelty...

our minds often search for an explanation.

Especially when we're young.

Children and teenagers have very little control over the adults around them.

So the mind reaches for the one thing it *can* control.

"Often it decides..."

(He looked back at Rudhika.)

"...'It must be me.'

'If I were better...

quieter...

smarter...

more obedient...

maybe they would have loved me.'"

(Rudhika's eyes widened.)

She had never spoken those thoughts aloud.

Yet somehow...

he had described them almost exactly.

**Professor Vinay**: Those conclusions can feel convincing.

Not because they're true.

But because they give chaos a reason.

And a reason...

even a painful one...

can feel less frightening than believing the people around you acted unjustly.

(Rudhika sat quietly.

For years, she had believed that becoming invisible was wisdom.

Now, for the first time, she wondered whether it had simply been the safest way a frightened fourteen-year-old knew how to survive.)

The afternoon bell rang across the campus.

Neither of them moved.

Some conversations...

were more important than the timetable.

(The afternoon bell had long since faded.

The pathways that had been crowded an hour ago were now almost empty.

Only a few students remained scattered across the campus.)

(Rudhika leaned back against the bench.

She felt exhausted.

Not physically.

Emotionally.

As though she had been carrying a heavy box for years...

and had finally set it down.)

Neither of them spoke.

The silence no longer felt heavy.

It felt peaceful.

After several minutes, Professor Vinay spoke.

**Professor Vinay**: Thank you.

(Rudhika looked at him, surprised.)

**Rudhika**: For what?

**Professor Vinay**: For trusting me with your story.

Trust isn't given because someone is a professor.

It's given because someone feels safe.

I don't take that lightly.

(Rudhika lowered her eyes.)

She wasn't used to people thanking her for speaking.

Usually...

they thanked her for staying quiet.

Vinay continued.

**Professor Vinay**: May I share one observation?

Rudhika nodded.

**Professor Vinay**: Everything you've described...

your silence...

your hesitation...

your fear of getting close to people...

they all make sense.

Not because they're ideal.

But because somewhere in your life...

they protected you.

(Rudhika listened carefully.)

**Professor Vinay**: Imagine you're caught in a storm.

You build a small shelter to survive.

The shelter saves your life.

Years later...

the storm is over.

But you never leave.

What once protected you...

slowly becomes a prison.

(Rudhika looked toward the sky.)

The clouds had almost disappeared.

She smiled faintly.

**Rudhika**: So...

I'm still living inside the shelter.

Vinay smiled.

**Professor Vinay**: Perhaps.

And perhaps that's alright.

You don't tear down a shelter overnight.

You first walk outside long enough to realize...

the storm isn't always there anymore.

(Rudhika quietly absorbed the thought.)

For the first time...

she didn't feel ashamed of the person she had become.

She saw a frightened fourteen-year-old...

doing her best to survive.

Maybe...

that girl deserved understanding...

not judgment.

A gentle breeze rustled the banyan leaves above them.

Rudhika watched them dance for a moment before speaking again.

**Rudhika**: Professor...

there's something I still don't understand.

(Vinay turned toward her.)

**Rudhika**: Why are people like that?

Why do some people enjoy hurting others?

Why are some homes filled with fear instead of love?

Why do people become so abusive?

Why do people hate someone simply because they're different...

or because they belong to another religion...

another caste...

another country...

another group?

Why does identity become more important than humanity?

(She paused.)

**Rudhika**: Were my grandparents always like that?

Was my cousin born that way?

Or...

did something make them become those people?

(The questions lingered in the evening air.)

Professor Vinay didn't answer immediately.

Instead, he looked across the campus where students from countless different backgrounds laughed together on the lawn.

Then he looked back at Rudhika.

A gentle smile crossed his face.

**Professor Vinay**: Those...

are some of the oldest questions in psychology.

And perhaps...

some of the oldest questions in human history.

(Rudhika waited.)

**Professor Vinay**: If I answer them in five minutes...

I'll only give you opinions.

They deserve something better.

They deserve careful thought.

(He stood, picking up the book he had brought but never opened.)

**Professor Vinay**: Tomorrow...

we'll begin exploring why people become who they become.

We'll talk about childhood...

learning...

fear...

identity...

power...

love...

and hate.

Not to excuse cruelty.

But to understand it.

Because understanding why something exists...

is the first step toward knowing how to prevent it.

(Rudhika stood as well.)

For the first time since she had entered college...

she wasn't looking forward to tomorrow because of a class.

She was looking forward to a conversation.

The sun slipped below the horizon.

Together...

they walked toward the college gate.

Not as a professor and his student.

But as two people...

trying to understand the human mind.

**End of Chapter 4**
