# When Words Become Weapons

(The next morning, the college campus buzzed with its usual energy.

Students hurried between lectures.

Some laughed with friends.

Others rushed toward classrooms, hoping they weren't late.

As Rudhika walked down the main corridor carrying her psychology notebook, raised voices caught her attention.)

A small crowd had gathered nearby.

Two students stood face to face.

One tried to explain something.

The other wasn't listening.

Instead...

he laughed.

"Oh, so now you're pretending to be an expert?"

Several students around them chuckled.

The first student's face reddened.

He tried to speak again.

Before he could finish...

the other interrupted.

"Stop embarrassing yourself."

More laughter.

(The conversation ended before it ever had a chance to begin.)

The first student quietly picked up his notebook and walked away.

The crowd slowly dispersed.

No one seemed particularly affected.

Except Rudhika.

(She stood there for several seconds.)

She hadn't been the one insulted.

Yet something about the scene made her uncomfortable.

It wasn't disagreement.

It wasn't criticism.

It was the enjoyment of humiliating another person.

(The incident stayed in her mind throughout the day.)

When Professor Vinay's psychology lecture ended, she waited as she always did until the other students had left.

(Vinay noticed the thoughtful expression on her face before she spoke.)

**Professor Vinay**: That's the same expression you had after yesterday's class.

**Rudhika**: (Smiling faintly.) Is it that obvious?

**Professor Vinay**: I'm a psychology professor.

Noticing things is part of the job.

(Rudhika laughed softly, but the smile quickly faded.)

They stepped into the corridor.

For a few moments, neither of them spoke.

Finally...

Rudhika broke the silence.

**Rudhika**: Professor...

Yesterday we talked about people who genuinely believe they're right.

People who defend their beliefs because they think they're protecting the truth.

(Vinay nodded.)

Rudhika stopped walking.

**Rudhika**: But what about people...

who don't seem interested in truth at all?

People who interrupt...

mock...

insult...

or humiliate others...

not because they're searching for understanding...

but simply because they enjoy hurting people.

(She looked directly at him.)

**Rudhika**: How do we deal with people who don't respect us enough to even have an honest conversation?

(Vinay remained silent for a few moments.

He didn't seem surprised by the question.

Instead...

he looked at the empty corridor stretching ahead of them.)

**Professor Vinay**: Before I answer...

tell me something.

When that student was being insulted this morning...

who did you feel sorry for?

(Rudhika answered without hesitation.)

**Rudhika**: The student who was being mocked.

**Professor Vinay**: Naturally.

And what did you feel toward the other student?

**Rudhika**: Honestly?

**Professor Vinay**: Always.

**Rudhika**: I was angry.

He wasn't even trying to understand.

He just wanted everyone to laugh.

(Vinay nodded.)

**Professor Vinay**: That's a very human reaction.

But let me ask you another question.

Who controlled your emotions today?

(Rudhika frowned.)

**Rudhika**: What do you mean?

**Professor Vinay**: You weren't part of the argument.

Yet someone else's behavior occupied your thoughts for hours.

(Rudhika became quiet.)

**Professor Vinay**: Think carefully.

Who decided how you would feel today?

The student...

or you?

(Rudhika looked down.)

She wanted to say, "I did."

But it didn't feel completely true.

(Vinay noticed her hesitation.)

**Professor Vinay**: Most people believe insults hurt because of the words themselves.

But words are only sounds...

until our minds give them meaning.

(Rudhika looked up.)

**Rudhika**: But Professor...

some words *do* hurt.

**Professor Vinay**: They do.

I'm not denying that.

I'm asking...

*why* they hurt.

(The question lingered.)

(Vinay bent down and picked up a dry twig from the ground.

He held it out toward Rudhika.)

**Professor Vinay**: If I call this twig...

a diamond...

does it become one?

**Rudhika**: No.

**Professor Vinay**: Why not?

**Rudhika**: Because calling something by another name doesn't change what it is.

(Vinay smiled.)

Then he gently pointed the twig toward her.

**Professor Vinay**: Suppose I call you...

"worthless."

Does that sentence change who you are?

(Rudhika instinctively shook her head.)

**Rudhika**: No...

**Professor Vinay**: Does it change your abilities?

**Rudhika**: No.

**Professor Vinay**: Your character?

**Rudhika**: No.

**Professor Vinay**: Your kindness?

**Rudhika**: No.

**Professor Vinay**: Then what has actually changed?

(Rudhika remained silent.)

**Professor Vinay**: Only this...

Someone has expressed an opinion.

Nothing more.

Nothing less.

(Rudhika wasn't fully convinced.)

**Rudhika**: But if it's only an opinion...

why does it feel so real?

(Vinay's expression softened.)

**Professor Vinay**: Because we're social beings.

From childhood...

our brains learn to care deeply about how others see us.

Acceptance once meant survival.

Rejection once meant danger.

Even today...

a harsh insult can awaken emotions that are thousands of years older than civilization itself.

So if insults affect you...

it doesn't mean you're weak.

It means you're human.

(Rudhika let out a slow breath.)

For some reason...

hearing that made her feel lighter.

**Professor Vinay**: But being human...

doesn't mean remaining helpless.

That's where wisdom begins.

(Rudhika looked at him with renewed curiosity.)

**Rudhika**: Then...

how do we become wiser than our first reaction?

(Vinay smiled.)

**Professor Vinay**: By asking one question before reacting.

Just one.

(Rudhika waited.)

**Professor Vinay**: "Is this person trying to help me...

or merely trying to hurt me?"

Because those are two very different conversations.

And they deserve two very different responses.

(Rudhika walked quietly beside Professor Vinay.

Students passed by them in both directions, but neither of them seemed to notice.

Her mind was entirely occupied by one thought.)

**Rudhika**: Professor...

how do I know which one it is?

Whether someone is trying to help me...

or simply trying to hurt me?

(Vinay smiled.)

**Professor Vinay**: Excellent.

That's exactly the question you should ask.

Not...

"Does this hurt?"

But...

"What is this person's intention?"

(Rudhika nodded slowly.)

**Professor Vinay**: Let me give you a simple test.

Imagine two different people say exactly the same sentence to you.

"Your presentation wasn't very clear."

Should you react the same way to both?

**Rudhika**: I suppose... yes?

**Professor Vinay**: Let's see.

Imagine the first person is your closest friend.

After everyone leaves, they quietly tell you,

"I know you worked hard.

I just think you explained the second topic too quickly."

Why did they wait until everyone left?

**Rudhika**: Because they didn't want to embarrass me.

**Professor Vinay**: Exactly.

Now imagine another student says the same thing.

But this time...

they say it loudly in front of the whole class.

Then they laugh.

Did the words change?

(Rudhika thought for a moment.)

**Rudhika**: No.

**Professor Vinay**: Then what changed?

**Rudhika**: The intention.

**Professor Vinay**: Precisely.

One person wanted to improve you.

The other wanted to display you.

One corrected.

The other humiliated.

Never confuse the two.

(Rudhika wrote another sentence in her notebook.)

> "Judge criticism by its intention, not only by its words."

(Vinay glanced at the page.)

**Professor Vinay**: That's a good sentence.

But let's make it even better.

(Rudhika looked up.)

**Professor Vinay**: Sometimes...

even a person with good intentions can be mistaken.

And sometimes...

a rude person accidentally speaks the truth.

(Rudhika blinked.)

**Rudhika**: I never thought of that.

**Professor Vinay**: That's why wise people ask two separate questions.

First...

"Is it true?"

Second...

"How was it said?"

Never reverse the order.

(Rudhika frowned.)

**Rudhika**: Why?

(Vinay stopped walking.)

He looked at the flowerbed beside the pathway.

A rose bush grew there.

Beautiful flowers.

Sharp thorns.

He gently pointed toward it.

**Professor Vinay**: Tell me...

would you refuse a rose...

simply because it has thorns?

**Rudhika**: No.

**Professor Vinay**: Would you grab the thorns...

because they're attached to a rose?

(Rudhika laughed.)

**Rudhika**: Of course not.

**Professor Vinay**: Exactly.

Take the flower.

Leave the thorns.

If criticism contains truth...

keep the truth.

Leave the cruelty behind.

If it contains only cruelty...

there's nothing to carry home.

(Rudhika smiled.)

She looked at the rose bush differently now.

**Professor Vinay**: The mistake many people make...

is carrying the thorns for years...

while leaving the flower behind.

(A gentle breeze carried the fragrance of the roses through the garden.)

For a few moments...

neither of them spoke.

Then Rudhika asked quietly,

**Rudhika**: Professor...

what if the person insulting me is someone I love?

A friend...

a parent...

or someone whose opinion matters to me?

Doesn't that make it much harder?

(Vinay's expression grew thoughtful.)

He nodded slowly.

**Professor Vinay**: Yes.

Much harder.

Because strangers can insult your ears.

But people you love...

can reach your heart.

(He looked at Rudhika with unusual seriousness.)

**Professor Vinay**: And that's where psychology alone isn't enough.

That conversation requires compassion...

boundaries...

and sometimes...

forgiveness.

(Rudhika sensed that this answer came not from a textbook...

but from experience.)

She closed her notebook.

She knew today's lesson was far from over.

(The afternoon sun had begun its slow descent.

Long shadows stretched across the garden paths.)

(Rudhika walked in silence.

She replayed every sentence Professor Vinay had spoken.

She understood them.

At least...

she thought she did.

Yet one question refused to leave her mind.)

**Rudhika**: Professor...

may I be honest?

(Vinay smiled.)

**Professor Vinay**: I would be disappointed if you weren't.

(Rudhika took a slow breath.)

**Rudhika**: I understand everything you've said.

I really do.

But...

it still hurts.

Even when I know someone is wrong.

Even when I know they're only trying to insult me...

it still hurts.

Why?

(Vinay's smile faded into a thoughtful expression.)

For several seconds...

he didn't answer.

Instead, he looked toward the horizon where the golden sunlight filtered through the trees.

**Professor Vinay**: Rudhika...

have you ever fallen while learning to ride a bicycle?

**Rudhika**: (Surprised.)

Many times.

**Professor Vinay**: Did knowing it was only a fall...

stop your knees from hurting?

(Rudhika smiled.)

**Rudhika**: No.

**Professor Vinay**: Exactly.

Understanding pain...

doesn't erase pain.

It simply helps you respond to it more wisely.

(Rudhika listened carefully.)

**Professor Vinay**: Psychology is often misunderstood.

People think it teaches us how to stop feeling.

It doesn't.

It teaches us how not to become prisoners of our feelings.

(The sentence lingered.)

**Rudhika**: So...

feeling hurt isn't failure?

**Professor Vinay**: Not at all.

Feeling is automatic.

What you do next...

is your choice.

(A young boy chased a football across the college ground nearby.

He stumbled...

fell...

and immediately looked around.)

Rudhika noticed something.

The boy wasn't checking his scraped knee.

He was checking whether anyone had seen him fall.

She smiled.

(Vinay noticed.)

**Professor Vinay**: What did you just observe?

**Rudhika**: He didn't care about the injury first.

He cared about being embarrassed.

(Vinay nodded.)

**Professor Vinay**: That's a psychologist's observation.

(Rudhika couldn't hide her smile.)

**Professor Vinay**: Sometimes...

the wound isn't nearly as painful...

as the story we tell ourselves about the wound.

(Rudhika repeated the sentence softly.)

"The story we tell ourselves..."

**Professor Vinay**: Imagine two people hear exactly the same insult.

One thinks,

"Maybe they're right.

I'm worthless."

The other thinks,

"That was unkind.

I wonder why they're so angry."

Same words.

Different stories.

Different suffering.

(Rudhika slowed her pace.)

She had never separated the insult...

from the story that followed it.

**Professor Vinay**: We rarely suffer only because of what happened.

We also suffer because of the meaning we attach to what happened.

(Rudhika stopped walking.)

**Rudhika**: Then...

is changing the story...

the same as lying to ourselves?

(Vinay smiled.)

A pleased smile.

Not because the question was easy.

But because it was exactly the right question.

**Professor Vinay**: No.

Lying ignores reality.

Wisdom looks at reality...

without adding unnecessary suffering.

If someone insults you...

the reality is,

"They insulted me."

The unnecessary story is,

"Therefore, I have no value."

Those are not the same sentence.

(Rudhika looked down.

She realized how often she had unknowingly joined those two sentences together.)

The breeze gently turned a page of her notebook.

On the page were all the lessons she had written over the last two days.

Questions.

Observations.

Ideas.

She smiled quietly.

Perhaps...

she wasn't only learning psychology anymore.

Perhaps...

she was learning how to understand herself.

(Vinay noticed the thoughtful look on Rudhika's face.

He slowed his pace.)

**Professor Vinay**: There's something else you should know.

(Rudhika looked up.)

**Professor Vinay**: The same words can affect two people very differently.

One person hears an insult...

shrugs...

and moves on.

Another carries those same words for years.

**Rudhika**: Why?

**Professor Vinay**: Because pain isn't measured only by what is said.

It's also shaped by who we are...

how we were raised...

what we've experienced...

what we've lost...

and what we've learned to believe about ourselves.

(Rudhika listened quietly.)

**Professor Vinay**: Psychology can explain a great deal about human behavior.

But no theory can completely explain a person.

Every individual carries a different story.

Different memories.

Different wounds.

Different strengths.

That's why understanding someone begins long before giving them advice.

(Rudhika lowered her eyes.

His words felt strangely personal.)

**Professor Vinay**: Which is why...

I don't think our next conversation should be about "people."

I think...

it should be about you.

(Rudhika looked up almost immediately.)

**Rudhika**: M-me?

(Vinay smiled gently.)

**Professor Vinay**: Yes.

You've spent the last few days trying to understand the human mind.

Tomorrow...

if you're comfortable with it...

I'd like to understand yours.

(Rudhika didn't know what to say.)

For a brief moment, the world around her seemed unusually quiet.

Students walked past them.

A bicycle bell rang in the distance.

Someone called out to a friend across the courtyard.

Yet all of it faded into the background.

No one had ever asked to understand her before.

People had asked about her grades.

Her attendance.

Her assignments.

Her future.

But never...

her.

(A faint warmth spread across her cheeks.)

She quickly looked away, pretending to adjust the strap of her bag.

**Rudhika**: (Softly.) I... I don't know if my story is interesting.

**Professor Vinay**: (With a kind smile.) Every mind is interesting.

The question is whether someone is patient enough to listen.

(Rudhika felt an unfamiliar feeling.

It wasn't embarrassment alone.

It was relief.)

(Vinay glanced at his watch.)

**Professor Vinay**: It's getting late.

Let's continue tomorrow.

No theories.

No textbooks.

Just a conversation.

(Rudhika nodded.)

This time, her smile came effortlessly.

(As Professor Vinay walked toward the faculty building, Rudhika remained where she was for a few moments.

The garden was beginning to empty.

She looked down at her notebook.

For the first time since buying it...

she wondered whether the next page would contain lessons from a professor...

or pieces of her own life.

She gently closed the notebook and walked toward the college gate.

Tomorrow no longer felt like another class.

It felt like the beginning of being understood.)

**End of Chapter 3**
